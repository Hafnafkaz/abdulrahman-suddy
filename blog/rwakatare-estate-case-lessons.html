The Rwakatare estate case: why your company's property is not your personal estate

A founder builds a church, schools and a company. She dies without a will. Years later the Court of Appeal has to answer one question: whose property is it?

Abdulrahman SuddyBusiness & Financial Advisory · Last updated: 19 November 2026 · 8 min read
Quick answerBishop Getrude Rwakatare died in 2020 without a will. Her children were appointed administrators, and a dispute arose over whether several properties belonged to her estate or to St Mary's International Academy Limited, her school company. In 2026 the Court of Appeal held that when a shareholder dies, heirs inherit the shares, not the company's assets. Properties registered in the company's name were removed from the estate, while a plot in her personal name stayed in it. The lesson: keep personal and company property clearly separate, and plan who will own the company after you.

Who was Bishop Rwakatare?

Bishop Getrude Rwakatare was a well-known Tanzanian church leader and former Member of Parliament. She founded the Mlima wa Moto church in Mikocheni and the St Mary's International Academy schools. She died in 2020.

What happened after her death?

According to The Citizen, she died without a will. Her children were appointed administrators of the estate. The final accounts they prepared listed many items, including shares and property linked to several institutions. One of her daughters objected. She said that several of the listed properties belonged to the school company, St Mary's International Academy Limited, and not to her mother's personal estate. She also said the company had not been a party to the court case.

What did the courts decide?

  • High Court. It dismissed the objection, finding that it had not been proved that the disputed properties belonged to the company.
  • Court of Appeal. In 2026 the Court of Appeal disagreed. It held that the High Court was wrong to treat the assets of a company that was not a party as part of the estate. It stated the principle that when a shareholder dies, the heirs inherit the shares in the company, not the company's own assets.

The properties registered in the company's name were removed from the estate, leaving the late bishop's shareholding in it. One plot that was registered in her own name stayed in the estate. Each side bore its own costs.

The legal principle in plain words

A company is a separate legal person. It owns its own property. The people who own the company own shares, and shares are what pass to heirs when an owner dies. So if a building is registered in the company's name, it belongs to the company, even if the founder started it, funded it and treated it as her own.

That sounds technical, but it is an everyday reality for founders. It means that who owns what depends on how things are registered, not on how the family has always spoken about them.

Five lessons for founders

  1. Write a will, even if you have a company. The company does not remove the need for one. Your shares, your personal property and your wishes still need a clear plan.
  2. Make titles match your intentions. If a building should belong to the company, register it in the company's name. If it should be yours, register it in yours. Do not leave it blurred.
  3. Keep proper company records. Share registers, resolutions and filings should show clearly who owns the company.
  4. Decide who runs the company after you. Inheriting shares is not the same as being able to manage a school, a church-linked business or a factory. A shareholders' agreement can set rules for ownership and management.
  5. Involve every heir in the process. Disputes often start when someone feels left out of the list of assets or the way it was prepared.

Where a holding company and a trust fit in

For founders with several ventures, a holding company can sit above them and make ownership clear, with each business owning its own assets. A trust can then hold the shares of the holding company for the family, with trustees who manage it. That gives the family one clear block of ownership and a plan for control, instead of a long list of items to argue over. It still needs a will alongside it. What to do this month

  • List every property and business interest you have, and who is named on each title or register.
  • Mark anything where the name does not match your intention.
  • Ask an advocate how to correct it and how to protect the company after you.
  • Write or update your will.

Frequently asked questions

What did the Court of Appeal decide in the Rwakatare estate case?

According to The Citizen, the Court of Appeal held that properties registered in the name of St Mary's International Academy Limited were the company's, not part of the late bishop's personal estate. Heirs inherit her shares, not the company's assets. A plot registered in her own name remained in the estate.

Do heirs inherit a company's property in Tanzania?

No. Heirs inherit the shares of the person who died. The company keeps owning its own property, because a company is a separate legal person.

Why is it important to separate company and personal property?

Ownership depends on how property is registered. If personal and company property are mixed, the family may end up in court deciding what belongs to whom.

Did Bishop Rwakatare leave a will?

The Citizen reported that she died without a will, and her children were appointed administrators of the estate.

How can a founder protect a family company?

Write a will, register property in the right name, keep company records up to date, sign a shareholders' agreement and consider a holding company and a trust to hold the shares.
