Succession planning

Succession planning in Tanzania: a practical guide for owners and families

Succession planning is not about death. It is about making sure the people you love, and the business you built, are looked after when you are not there to explain.

Abdulrahman SuddyBusiness & Financial Advisory · Last updated: 19 November 2026 · 12 min read
Quick answerSuccession planning means deciding now who will own, run and benefit from your assets after you. In Tanzania the starting point is a valid will, drafted with an advocate and kept safe. If you die without a will, your heirs must apply to the court to appoint an administrator, and your estate is shared under the law that applies to you, which can depend on your faith and way of life. Business owners should also plan who will own and run the company, using tools such as a shareholders' agreement, a holding company and, in some cases, a trust.

What is succession planning?

It is the plan for what happens to your assets, your family and your business when you die or can no longer decide for yourself. For a family it answers: who inherits what, who looks after the children, who handles the paperwork. For an owner it also answers: who owns the company, who runs it, and how it keeps going.

What happens if you die without a will in Tanzania?

If there is no will, the law decides for you, and the process usually follows this path.

  1. The family chooses someone to administer the estate. In practice this often happens at a family or clan meeting, which commonly leads to written minutes. Courts have said the meeting is practice, not a strict legal requirement, but it helps reduce disputes.
  2. That person applies to the court for letters of administration, with documents such as the death certificate and an affidavit.
  3. The court usually appoints the administrator unless someone raises a serious objection.
  4. The administrator collects the assets, pays debts, prepares an inventory and shares what remains among the heirs under the law that applies.

This can take a long time and can be tense. Old family grievances often surface at exactly this point. If there are no heirs at all, the estate can pass to the government through the Administrator-General.

Which law decides who inherits?

Tanzania has more than one system, and which applies can depend on a person's faith and way of life.

  • Islamic law generally applies to Muslims. Courts have looked at whether the deceased actually lived as a Muslim. Islamic rules fix shares for certain heirs and limit how much can be left by will outside those shares.
  • Customary law can apply to some families and communities.
  • General law (including rules based on the Indian Succession Act) applies in other cases, such as many Christians.

Women and daughters have sometimes been treated unequally under customary and religious rules, which is another reason to plan carefully. Ask an advocate which rules apply to you and how far your will can go.

What does a will do, and what makes it valid?

A will states who inherits, who the executor is, and who looks after minor children. A will that is badly made can be challenged and set aside. Tanzanian courts look at things such as whether the will was properly signed and sealed, whether the signature looks genuine, whether it was properly witnessed (sources note the rules can require witnesses including a relative), and whether the person had the capacity to make it. Always have an advocate prepare it.

  • Choose an executor you trust, and a back-up.
  • Keep the original safe. Options include your advocate, a bank, the High Court or RITA.
  • Update it after a marriage, birth, death, divorce or major change in your business.

Why do so many families end up in court?

Experts quoted in Tanzanian reporting point to the same causes again and again: no will, interference from relatives, old tensions that were never discussed, and a widespread belief that writing a will invites death. A psychologist put it well: a will is responsible planning, not a prediction. The fix is simple and uncomfortable: talk about it while everyone is alive.

How do you plan succession for a family business?

  1. Separate ownership from management. The person who inherits shares is not always the person who should run the company.
  2. Know what is personal and what is the company's. Courts treat a company as its own legal person. Heirs inherit the shares, not the company's assets. Keep titles and records clean.
  3. Put agreements in writing. A shareholders' agreement can cover what happens when an owner dies, who can buy the shares and how they are valued.
  4. Consider a holding company. It puts your shares and assets in one place and makes passing them on simpler. See holding companies in Tanzania.
  5. Consider a trust. Trustees can hold the shares for the family so the business stays together. See trusts in Tanzania.
  6. Prepare the next generation. Involve them early, and be honest about roles.
  7. Keep company records in order. That includes the share register and beneficial ownership information filed with BRELA.

A ten-step succession plan you can start this month

  1. List everything you own and how each item is registered.
  2. List who depends on you.
  3. Find out which succession rules apply to you.
  4. Decide who should get what, and who should manage it.
  5. Choose an executor and a back-up.
  6. Have an advocate draft your will.
  7. Fix any mismatch between personal and company property.
  8. Decide whether a holding company or trust makes sense.
  9. Tell your executor and key family members where the papers are.
  10. Review everything every year or after any big life change.

Lessons from Tanzanian cases

Two widely reported cases show the stakes. A prominent businessman left a will that the High Court set aside, partly because of how it was made. A founder died without a will, and the courts later had to decide what belonged to her company and what belonged to her estate. Read the Mengi will case and the Rwakatare estate case.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if I die without a will in Tanzania?

Your heirs must appoint an administrator, usually through a family meeting, and apply to the court for letters of administration. The administrator collects the assets, pays debts and shares the rest under the law that applies to you. This can be slow and can cause family disputes.

Do I need a lawyer to write a will in Tanzania?

It is strongly advisable. A will that is not properly signed, witnessed and drafted can be challenged and set aside, as happened in a widely reported High Court case.

Where should I keep my will?

Common options are with your advocate, with a bank, with the High Court or with RITA. Tell your executor where it is.

Can a will be challenged in Tanzania?

Yes. Relatives and others can object to a will, for example over its form, over whether the person had capacity, or over whether it complies with the law that applies.

Which law applies to my estate?

It depends on factors such as your faith and way of life. Islamic, customary and general law can each apply. An advocate can tell you which applies to you.

What is the difference between an executor and an administrator?

An executor is named in your will. An administrator is appointed by the court when there is no will, or when the will names no executor.

How do I protect my family business after I die?

Write a will, keep company and personal property separate, sign a shareholders' agreement, and consider a holding company or a trust to hold the shares.
