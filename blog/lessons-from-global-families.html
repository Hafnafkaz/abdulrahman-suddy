What Alphabet, Tata and IKEA Teach Tanzanian Founders About Ownership

The biggest groups in the world did not grow by accident. They decided early who owns what, who controls it, and what happens when the founder is gone.

Abdulrahman SuddyBusiness & Financial Advisory · 19 November 2026 · 7 min read

Structure is rarely discussed at the point where a business is small, and it is the point where it is cheapest to get right. Here are four well-known examples, what problem each solved, and what is practical for a Tanzanian owner under local law.

Alphabet: separate the bets

In 2015 Google reorganised under a new parent, Alphabet. The core search and advertising business sat in Google, and other ventures sat in their own companies under the parent. Leaders could be held to account separately, and capital could be allocated between the units.

The idea for Tanzania: if you run a profitable core business and also want to start other ventures, put the ventures in their own companies under a parent. If one fails, the others are not automatically dragged down. See the holding company guide for what this costs here.

Berkshire Hathaway: let the parent be an owner, not an operator

Berkshire is a holding company that owns businesses such as GEICO and BNSF Railway. The parent allocates capital, and the subsidiaries are run by their own managers.

The idea for Tanzania: a holding company works best when the owner thinks like an investor, taking profit up from the operating company and reinvesting it. In Tanzania the dividend into a holdco from a 25%-or-more shareholding is withheld at 5% against 10% for an individual, so reinvestment has a modest tax advantage.

Tata: let a trust own the company

Tata Sons, the holding company of the Tata group, is majority-owned by philanthropic trusts, widely reported at about two-thirds. Profits flow to causes, and the group's ownership cannot be easily broken up or sold by individual heirs.

The idea for Tanzania: the lesson is that ownership can be separated from family inheritance. The practical route here is limited. Charitable and community purposes can use Registered Trustees under the Trustees' Incorporation Act, but a private family trust that holds shares depends on the trust deed rather than a dedicated statute, so you need an advocate and a clear purpose.

IKEA: a foundation holds the shares

Ingka Group, the largest IKEA retailer, is owned by Stichting INGKA Foundation, a Dutch foundation. The structure is designed to keep the business independent and long-term.

The idea for Tanzania: some countries have foundation laws that Tanzania does not match. You cannot copy the structure. You can copy the principle, which is to write down, while you are alive, who controls the company after you and why.

What this means for you

  • Decide before you scale. Moving shares later costs stamp duty and can trigger tax. Getting it right early is cheap.
  • Separate ownership from control. A shareholders' agreement can state who runs the company even when shares pass to heirs.
  • Put it in writing. The common thread in all four is a decision recorded in documents, not a hope.
  • Use local law, not borrowed structures. Take the idea and ask a Tanzanian advocate for the version that works here.
