The Mengi will case: what every Tanzanian business owner can learn

One of Tanzania's best-known businessmen left a will. The High Court still set it aside. Here is what was reported, and why it matters if you own a business or a family home.

Abdulrahman SuddyBusiness & Financial Advisory · Last updated: 19 November 2026 · 8 min read
Quick answerReginald Mengi died in 2019. A will presented after his death was challenged, and in 2021 the High Court declared it invalid. The reasons reported included how the will was made and signed, concerns about his health and capacity, and the exclusion of some children. The court directed that the estate be handled as if there were no will. The Court of Appeal later refused the widow's request for a review, so the High Court decision stands. The lesson: a will is only as strong as its form, its timing and its fairness, and owners should plan early.

A note on how we write about this. This is a public court matter reported in the Tanzanian press. Families have their own views and the matter has been painful for many people. We are not taking sides or judging anyone. We use the case only to show what can go wrong in succession planning, so others can avoid it. Details come from news reports and may have moved on since.

Who was Reginald Mengi?

Reginald Mengi was one of the best-known business leaders in Tanzania, with interests in media, manufacturing and more. He died in Dubai in May 2019.

What happened after his death?

A will was presented in court. Four people asked to be appointed to act under it. Two close relatives, his son Abdiel and his brother Benjamin, objected to its validity. His widow tried to take part in the case but was refused by the court.

Why did the High Court set the will aside?

According to reporting by The Citizen, the objections that the court accepted included the following.

  • Form. Questions over whether the will was sealed, whether the signature matched his usual one, and whether it was witnessed by a relative or his wife.
  • Capacity. Evidence that he had suffered a stroke in 2016 and had not recovered, which raised doubts about whether he could make sound decisions about his estate.
  • Exclusion. The will left nothing to children from his first marriage, without explanation, and was said to include property that came from his first wife.

The judge declared that the will did not meet the legal requirements to be treated as valid. The court did not appoint the people who had applied under the will. It appointed his son Abdiel and his brother Benjamin to administer the estate, to be distributed as if he had died without a will.

What happened next?

The legal process continued for years. The Court of Appeal considered further applications. In 2024 it dismissed the widow's request for a review of its decision, saying the reasons given were grounds for an appeal rather than a review. The result is that the High Court ruling stands and the estate is being administered under the no-will process. How far the sharing has progressed is not clear from public reports.

What this case does and does not tell us

  • It does not mean every will fails. Most valid, properly made wills are respected.
  • It does not tell us the intentions of anyone involved.
  • Several of the Court of Appeal rulings dealt with procedure, not the full merits.

What it does show is how many things can be attacked when a will is made late, made while someone is unwell, and leaves out people who expected a share.

Five lessons for business owners

  1. Get the form right. Use an advocate. Follow the formalities for signing, sealing and witnessing, including the witness rules that apply, so there is nothing technical to attack.
  2. Plan while you are healthy. Documents signed years before illness are far harder to question than documents signed during it. If a will must be made when health is poor, get a doctor's assessment of capacity recorded at the time.
  3. Deal honestly with every branch of the family. If you have children from more than one relationship, explain your reasons in writing, or provide for everyone in some way. Silence invites a challenge.
  4. Separate what belongs to whom. Make sure it is clear which assets came from an earlier marriage, which are shared and which are yours alone.
  5. Talk to your family while you can. Surprise is the most expensive thing in succession.

Where a trust or holding company fits in

No structure is immune to challenge, and no one can say what a different plan would have changed in this case. But structures set up years earlier, while the owner is healthy and with independent advice, give a family more to stand on than a single last-minute document. A holding company can put business interests in one place with clear ownership, and a trust can name trustees to hold and manage them for the family. Both should still sit alongside a properly made will. Read more in our guides to trusts in Tanzania and succession planning.

What to do this month

  • Find out whether you have a valid will, and where it is.
  • If it was made in a hurry or a long time ago, have an advocate review it.
  • List your assets and how each one is registered.
  • Decide who should know your plan, and tell them.

Frequently asked questions

Why was Reginald Mengi's will declared invalid?

According to reports, the High Court found that the will did not meet the legal requirements. The reasons reported included questions about its form and signature, concerns about his capacity after a stroke, and the exclusion of some of his children.

Who is administering the Mengi estate?

The High Court appointed his son Abdiel and his brother Benjamin to administer the estate, to be distributed as if there were no will. Check recent reports, because the situation may have changed.

Can a will be challenged in Tanzania?

Yes. Relatives and others can object to a will over its form, the maker's capacity, or whether it complies with the law that applies.

Would a trust have prevented this dispute?

No one can say. But a trust or holding structure set up earlier, with independent advice and clear records, usually gives a family a stronger footing than a single late will. It should still be combined with a valid will.

What can business owners learn from the Mengi case?

Use an advocate, follow the formalities, plan while healthy, deal openly with every branch of the family and keep personal and business property clearly separate.
