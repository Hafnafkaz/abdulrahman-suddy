Trust Deed in Tanzania: 15 Clauses Every Family Trust Needs

The trust deed is the trust. Registration gives it a legal identity, but only the deed says who benefits, what trustees must do and what happens when the family disagrees.

Abdulrahman SuddyBusiness & Financial Advisory · Last updated: 19 November 2026 · 8 min read
Quick answerA Tanzanian family trust deed should name the settlor, trustees and beneficiaries, describe the trust property, state the purpose, and set out trustees' powers and duties in clear, enforceable terms. It should cover accounting and reporting, distributions, investment, how trustees are appointed and removed, meetings and decisions, conflicts of interest, disputes, amendment and dissolution. Courts cannot enforce duties a deed never created, so precision matters more than length.

Why the deed matters more than anything else

In the 2024 High Court case involving the Alli Mberesero Foundation, a registered family foundation tried to make relatives account for properties they managed. The court found the duties had never been clearly created: family meeting minutes and an uncertain agreement were not enough. The lesson is blunt. If a duty is not written clearly, it may not exist. Read more in trustee duties and the Mberesero case.

The 15 clauses

  1. Name and date, including "Registered Trustees" if you will incorporate.
  2. The parties. Settlor, first trustees and the class of beneficiaries.
  3. Purpose. One or two sentences on what the trust is for: education, care, keeping the business together, family welfare.
  4. Trust property. A schedule of the initial assets, and a power to accept more later.
  5. Beneficiaries and their entitlements. Fixed shares, or discretion for trustees to decide within limits.
  6. Distributions. When and how money is paid out: ages, purposes, conditions, frequency.
  7. Trustee powers. To invest, lease, sell, borrow, open accounts, vote shares and appoint advisers.
  8. Trustee duties. Written as obligations: keep proper books, prepare annual accounts, report to beneficiaries, preserve property, remit income to the trust.
  9. Investment rules. What trustees may and may not invest in, and any need for professional advice.
  10. Appointment, retirement and removal of trustees, including who holds that power and what happens if a trustee dies.
  11. Decision-making. Quorum, voting, written resolutions and what happens on a deadlock.
  12. Conflicts of interest. Rules for trustees who are also beneficiaries or who deal with the trust.
  13. Records and accounts. What must be kept, for how long, and whether accounts are audited.
  14. Disputes. A ladder of family meeting, mediation, then arbitration or court.
  15. Amendment and dissolution. Who can change the deed, how, and where assets go if the trust ends.

Drafting principles

  • Write duties as duties. "The trustees shall prepare accounts within three months of each year end" beats "the trustees should keep the family informed".
  • Avoid uncertain words. Agreements whose meaning cannot be made certain can be void under Tanzanian contract law.
  • Match the deed to real assets. A deed that lists property the trust never received protects nothing.
  • Plan for the people, not just the assets. Think about death, incapacity, divorce and disagreement among trustees.

Deed and letter of wishes work together

Keep the deed precise and legally enforceable. Put softer guidance, such as your values or how you hope discretion will be used, in a separate letter of wishes.

Frequently asked questions

Who should draft a trust deed in Tanzania?

A Tanzanian advocate experienced in trusts and succession. The deed must be precise enough to be enforced and must fit the registration rules if you plan to incorporate.

Can I change the trust deed later?

Only if the deed allows it and only in the way it sets out. For registered trusts, changes to the deed must be notified to the Administrator-General within the time limit.

What happens if the trust deed is vague?

Duties that are not clearly created may not be enforceable, as the High Court found in the Mberesero case in 2024. Vagueness invites disputes.
