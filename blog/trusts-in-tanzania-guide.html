Trusts & estate planning

Trusts in Tanzania: how they work, why they help, and how to set one up

Most Tanzanian families have never been told that a trust exists. Those who have often hear that it is only for the very rich. Here is what a trust really does, and who it is for.

Abdulrahman SuddyBusiness & Financial Advisory · Last updated: 19 November 2026 · 12 min read
Quick answerA trust is a legal arrangement where you (the settlor) hand assets to people you trust (trustees) who must look after them for other people (beneficiaries) under written rules called a trust deed. In Tanzania, trusts work through the Trustees' Incorporation Act for registered trusts with religious, educational, social or charitable purposes, and through general trust law and the trust deed for private family trusts. A well-drafted trust can protect children, keep a business running and reduce family conflict. It does not replace a will, and it needs an advocate to draft it.

What is a trust, in simple words?

Think of a trust as a safe with rules. You put assets inside: shares, a building, savings. You choose the people who look after the safe (the trustees). You write the rules for who benefits and when (the trust deed). From then on, the trustees hold the assets and must use them for the beneficiaries, not for themselves.

There are three roles. The settlor creates the trust and puts assets in. The trustees hold the assets and manage them. The beneficiaries receive the benefit. One person can sometimes hold more than one role, but the clearer the split, the stronger the trust.

Does Tanzania recognise trusts?

Yes. Tanzania recognises trusts, but the law is thinner than in countries known for trusts. Two things are useful to know.

  • Registered trusts. The Trustees' Incorporation Act (Cap 318) lets trustees who hold property for religious, educational, literary, scientific, social or charitable purposes, or for a body bound together by custom, religion, kinship or nationality, apply to the Administrator-General (through RITA) to become a body corporate. The name must include "Registered Trustees". This is how many churches, mosques, schools and community groups hold property.
  • Private family trusts. For a trust that looks after your own family's wealth, Tanzanian law firms note that the arrangement rests mainly on the trust deed and on general principles of trust law (equity). There is no modern, dedicated statute for private family trusts. That is not a reason to avoid them. It is a reason to have the deed drafted carefully by an advocate.

What types of trust are used in Tanzania?

  • Family (living) trust. Created while you are alive. You can often keep a say in how it runs, depending on how the deed is written.
  • Testamentary trust. Created by your will and starting when you die. Useful for young children.
  • Registered Trustees. For charities, faith groups, schools and community bodies.
  • Business or shareholding trust. Trustees hold shares in the family company so the business stays together and is not split by inheritance.
  • Wakf. For Muslims, an endowment under Islamic law for religious, charitable or family benefit. Wakf has its own rules, so speak to an advocate who knows Islamic law.

How can a trust benefit you and your family?

  • Protection for children and vulnerable people. Money can be released over time, for school fees and care, instead of landing in a young person's hands at once.
  • Continuity. Assets already held by trustees are generally not part of your personal estate, so the family is not left waiting for the court to sort out who is in charge.
  • Fewer arguments. Written rules, decided while you are alive and healthy, leave less room for relatives to fight over what you meant.
  • Privacy. Estate proceedings in court are a public process. A trust deed is a private document.
  • A home for the family business. Trustees can hold the shares so the company is managed properly while the family shares the benefit.
  • Control beyond your lifetime. You can say when and why money is paid out, not just who gets it.

What a trust cannot do

  • It is not a tax trick. Trusts are about protection and order. Tax treatment depends on how the trust is set up, and transfers into a trust can attract stamp duty and other costs.
  • It does not work if it is empty. A trust only protects what has actually been transferred into it. Shares must be registered in the trustees' names. Land must be properly transferred and registered.
  • It cannot defeat the law. A trust set up to hide assets from creditors, or to dodge mandatory succession rules, can be challenged. Courts look at what really happened.
  • It does not replace a will. Anything you did not put into the trust still passes through your estate.
  • It cannot be better than its trustees. Pick people who are honest, organised and willing to act.

They work best together. A common pattern is a holding company that owns the businesses, a trust that owns the holding company shares, and a will that deals with everything else. Read our guide on holding companies in Tanzania and on succession planning.

Should you set up a trust? A quick test

A trust is worth a serious conversation with an advocate if two or more of these are true.

  • You have young children or a dependant who cannot manage money.
  • You own a business or valuable property that the family should not have to split.
  • You have children from more than one relationship.
  • You want assets managed for years, not handed over in one go.
  • You want your wishes written down while you can still explain them.

If none apply, a clear and valid will may be all you need today. Revisit when your life changes.

How to set up a trust in Tanzania, step by step

  1. Write your goal in one sentence. For example: "Keep the business together and pay for the children's education until they are adults."
  2. List what you own and how it is registered. Titles, share registers, bank accounts.
  3. Choose your trustees. At least two, plus a successor. Many families combine a trusted relative with a professional.
  4. Choose beneficiaries and say when they benefit. Be specific about ages, purposes and conditions.
  5. Instruct an advocate to draft the trust deed. It should cover trustee powers, duties, how trustees are replaced, and what happens if the family disagrees.
  6. Fund the trust properly. Transfer the assets and update the records: share register, land registry, bank mandates. Pay any stamp duty due.
  7. Write a letter of wishes. It is not legally binding, but it guides trustees on how you would want discretion used.
  8. Update your will. Make sure it works with the trust instead of fighting it.
  9. Tell the people involved. Trustees and key family members should know the trust exists and where the papers are.
  10. Review it regularly. After a birth, a death, a marriage, or a major change in the business.

How do you choose a good trustee?

Look for honesty first, then organisation, then judgement. Avoid appointing one person who is also the only beneficiary, because there is no check on them. A pair of trustees, one family member and one independent person, is a common and sensible balance. Always name a successor, so the trust does not stall if a trustee dies or steps down.

Common mistakes that cause trouble

  • Setting up a trust but never moving assets into it.
  • Using a vague deed that does not say who decides when trustees disagree.
  • Assuming a trust means you do not need a will.
  • Mixing personal and company property, so no one is sure what belongs where.
  • Ignoring the succession rules that apply to your faith and family.
  • Keeping the plan secret until it is too late to explain it.

What Tanzanian court cases teach about trusts

Two widely reported cases show what happens when planning is missing or unclear. In one, a will left by a prominent businessman was set aside by the High Court, and his estate is being handled as if there were no will. In the other, a founder died without a will and the courts later had to decide whether properties belonged to her company or to her estate. Read the lessons in the Mengi will case and the Rwakatare estate case.

What does it cost, and who should you speak to?

Costs depend on how complex your assets are, how the deed is drafted and what must be transferred or registered. Ask a Tanzanian advocate for a written quote and a clear list of what is included. A good advisor can help you work out the ownership structure first, so the advocate drafts the right documents the first time.

Frequently asked questions

Can I create a trust in Tanzania?

Yes. Trusts are recognised in Tanzania. Registered trusts for religious, educational, social or charitable purposes use the Trustees' Incorporation Act. Private family trusts rely mainly on the trust deed and general trust law, so the deed should be drafted by a Tanzanian advocate.

Is a trust better than a will in Tanzania?

They do different jobs. A will says who inherits when you die. A trust has trustees manage assets for beneficiaries, sometimes for many years. Most families need a will, and some also benefit from a trust.

Does a trust avoid probate in Tanzania?

Assets that were properly transferred into a trust during your lifetime are generally not part of your personal estate, so they do not need to go through probate. Anything left outside the trust still does, which is why you should keep a will as well.

Can a trust own land or shares in Tanzania?

Trustees can hold property for the beneficiaries. For land, the transfer must be registered at the land registry, and the Land Act has special rules for non-citizens. For shares, the trustees must be entered in the company's share register.

Can Muslims use trusts in Tanzania?

Muslims can use arrangements such as a wakf, which has its own rules under Islamic law. Islamic succession rules can also limit what can be given outside the fixed family shares, so take advice from an advocate who knows both Tanzanian and Islamic law.

Can I change or cancel a trust?

It depends on the trust deed. Some trusts allow the settlor to change or end them. Others are irrevocable once created. Decide this when the deed is drafted.

How do I choose between a trust and a holding company?

Use a holding company to organise ownership of your businesses and separate risk. Use a trust to decide who benefits and who looks after the assets for your family. Many owners use both.
